# Recording-led sound design

## Identity and material

For each recognizable entity/action, choose a lead mechanism and articulation before adding layers. Different identities can vary in physical material, attack shape, spectral weight, micro-rhythm, recovery mechanism and duration. Transposing one source is not a whole identity system. Pitch should follow the emitting mechanism and action: a tiny point-defense gun on a huge ship need not sound like a capital cannon.

Useful functional layers are transient/pressure, characteristic body, mechanism, and exit/environment. They need not all be present. Give each a spectral and temporal role; leave intentional gaps. The request for heavily processed detail is not a request for broadband hiss or a maximal layer count.

Use material recordings with documented rights. Retain original encoding; a decoded MP3 is not a recovered lossless original. Keep checksums, exact source/license URLs, recipes, seeds and output hashes. Fail on changed source content instead of silently accepting a new download. Use an original palette instead of film/game dialogue, music or ripped effects.

## Heavy processing with physical weight

Useful operations include authored varispeed curves, time manipulation, source-excited resonances, frequency shifting, filter movement, parallel multiband saturation, granular edits and short convolution. Print the result, edit its best gesture, then process again if useful. Each operation should explain a material or action; an arbitrary sweep is often perceived as a cartoon gesture.

Inspect post-processing resonances. A spring, metal panel or comb-filter body can remain bell-like after aggressive distortion. Repeatedly adding notches or another transient may leave the offending core intact. Replace that core with pressure-rich atonal material when it is the wrong source.

## Weapon shots: punch without cowbells

Use a broadband crack and compact pressure body as the principal event. Appropriate recorded impulse material can include licensed blasts, pops and fractures, transformed into an original mechanism. Select the actual onset rather than quiet echoes or arbitrary slices; never tile an attack to fill a missing tail. Keep a lower-weight recoil/latch layer if it helps recognition.

A sub-millisecond clean entrance can retain the crack; a generic long attack fade blunts it. A brief low-to-settled pitch contour can contribute weight, but inspect the result for musical ringing. Do not impose the same contour on every shot. Keep appreciable body after the first transient, often within tens of milliseconds, then release before the cadence becomes a sustained metallic tone.

For firing variants:

- Preserve pitch identity and body register across takes. No random octave/semitone swaps.
- Prefer subtle source-detail, gain and micro-timing changes over transposition.
- If detuning helps phase stacking, choose an almost imperceptible fixed value per event. An example is triangular +/-3 cents, not several percent playback-speed jitter plus another runtime detune.
- Audit source-window selection: different recorded hits can have different perceived notes even with identical playback rates.
- Listen to a repeated volley, not just shuffled isolated samples. Distinguish narrow resonances from legitimate low pressure/body.

Physical impact and UI confirmation are separate events. Hull hits can have randomized fractures and mechanical weight; a hitmarker should be a tiny, unobtrusive click. Enemy-versus-enemy contact needs world audio too. Death and large explosions deserve separate progression instead of louder copies of small hits.

## Research orientation

Use primary interviews for production principles and official engine documentation for APIs. Useful starting references:

- [Erik Aadahl on Transformers character identities](https://designingsound.org/2010/03/25/erik-aadahl-special-the-sound-design-of-transformers-exclusive-interview/)
- [Aadahl on recording and processing](https://designingsound.org/2010/03/30/erik-aadahl-special-reader-questions/)
- [War for Cybertron audio team](https://designingsound.org/2010/07/27/exclusive-interview-with-the-audio-team-of-transformers-war-for-cybertron/)
- [DICE's Ben Minto on game sound and mix modes](https://www.waves.com/ben-minto-on-game-sound-design)

These describe transferable processes. Do not claim to reproduce undocumented proprietary chains. Revisit current primary documentation when adopting new APIs or processors.
